Case study · Module design

Ethical and Transparent AI Use in College Composition

A one-to-two-week module that teaches undergraduate writers to evaluate AI-generated text, document their AI use and keep ownership of their arguments.

Audience
Undergraduate composition students, both native and non-native English speakers, with little prior instruction on ethical AI use
Format
Five sessions over 1–2 weeks. Works in person, hybrid or online.
My role
Sole designer: module outline, lesson plan and student materials
Tools
Canvas discussions, Google Docs, Mentimeter, Padlet, ChatGPT
Context
Course project, EDUC 5030 Designing Effective Learning Environments, Iowa State University
Year
2025

The challenge

AI-generated text is now part of how many students write, and composition instructors need ways to teach and assess writing with it present. The students this module serves have had little instruction on what acceptable AI use looks like.

Classes also mix native speakers and multilingual writers, who notice different things in AI text. The module had to support both groups while keeping academic integrity and students' confidence in their own writing at the center.

Module objectives

By the end of the module, students will be able to:

  1. Evaluate AI-generated text for accuracy, bias and rhetorical appropriateness in academic writing.
  2. Apply institutional and course AI-use guidelines by documenting their AI interactions transparently.
  3. Compose and revise an original argumentative essay that shows their own rhetorical decisions, using AI only as a constrained support tool.

Day 2 lesson: Evaluating AI-generated text

A 75-minute session, or two 50-minute sessions in hybrid and asynchronous versions.

  1. Activate10 min · Mentimeter poll: "Can you tell if writing is AI-generated?"
  2. Model15 min · Generate a paragraph live and annotate it beside an anonymized student paragraph
  3. Practice25 min · Groups annotate paired texts in Google Docs with the evaluation checklist
  4. Debrief10 min · Sort observations on Padlet: lexical range, bias, authorship tone
  5. Reflect10 min · What made the AI writing sound less authentic, and how will you use that?

Design decisions

Facilitation notes for the class in the room

Each part of the lesson has notes for classes that are mostly multilingual, mostly native speakers or mixed. With multilingual writers, the instructor models noticing academic collocations such as "play a role in." With native speakers, the focus shifts to sentence variety and stance.

Roles that keep group work balanced

In mixed groups, each student takes a role: tone reviewer, cohesion reviewer or evidence reviewer. The roles give every student a defined part, whatever their language background.

Analysis framed around credibility

The analysis is framed as a study of how language builds credibility in academic writing. Students judge what makes a text convincing, which transfers to their own drafts.

Documentation as a habit

Students start an AI-use log on Day 3, get feedback on it mid-unit, and annotate it with a reflection at the end. Transparency is practiced several times before it is graded.
